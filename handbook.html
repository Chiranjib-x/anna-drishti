Read this page first. It explains the evidence-classification system, without which the rest of the document can be misread — and misreading it in front of a judge is worse than not reading it at all.

The evidence classification system

Every technical claim in this handbook carries a tag. The tag tells you how much weight the claim can bear under questioning. This matters more than whether the claim sounds impressive: a judge who catches you stating an assumption as a fact will discount everything else you say.

TagWhat it meansHow to speak it
Established Textbook physics, chemistry or biology. Not in dispute. State it plainly. “Grain is an insulator.”
Literature Supported by a specific published source we can name. Name the source. “A 2023 study in Scientific Reports found…”
Engineering est. A defensible calculation from accepted constants, but our arithmetic, not a published result. Say it is modelled. “Our model gives roughly…”
Assumption A value we chose to make the model runnable. Reasonable, but chosen. Own it. “We assumed X, and here is why that is conservative.”
Hypothesis What we expect to happen but have not shown. Say “we expect”, never “we found”.
Needs validation Cannot be defended today. An honest gap. Volunteer it before you are asked.
The single rule that protects you

If a judge asks “how do you know?”, your answer must identify which of the six categories above the claim sits in. “That is an assumption we made, and here is why we chose it” is a strong answer. “It is in our model” is not an answer at all — it describes where the number lives, not why it is right.

Section 0The audit

Nine findings from checking every claim in the deck against the simulation source and the literature. Read this section even if you read nothing else. Four of these change what you should say out loud.

0.1   Findings that change your wording

Finding 1 — “physically cannot” is an overclaim (Slide 5)

The deck says: “0.82 — GROWTH FLOOR. Below this A. flavus physically cannot pull water out of the grain. Not ‘slow’ — cannot.”

The problem: reported minimum water activity for A. flavus growth varies by strain, substrate and temperature. Published values range from roughly 0.82 up to 0.91 depending on the study — one study on sorghum reported a minimum of 0.91 for mycelial growth, while classical work on corn proposed values around 0.83–0.84 for toxin production. 0.82 is the conservative lower end of a reported range, not a physical constant.

Why it matters: a mycology-literate judge knows the spread. The word “cannot” invites a correction that makes every other number you state look equally overconfident.

Say this instead: “0.82 is the conservative end of the published range for A. flavus growth — reported minima vary with strain and substrate, some studies report higher. We deliberately took the lowest credible value, because for a warning system you want to alert early rather than late.” That answer is stronger, because it shows you know the literature has spread and chose your side of it deliberately.

Finding 2 — the 1.6 m figure needs one qualifying word (Slide 7)

The deck says: “A spear reaches forty centimetres. Our hotspot is at one point six metres.”

What the model actually contains: the demo pocket sits at grid cell (24, 16) in a 4.0 × 2.5 m stack. Its distances to the four faces are:

Finding 3 — “~15×” understates your own model (Slide 3)

The deck says: “SO GAS LEADS BY ~15×”.

The model gives 16.7×, both as a ratio of arrival times (15.43 d ÷ 0.926 d) and as a ratio of diffusivities (2.0×10⁻⁶ ÷ 1.2×10⁻⁷). These agree exactly because arrival time scales as L²/D, so the length cancels.

This is a harmless rounding down — but know the exact figure, because “16.7, we round it down to about 15 when we speak” sounds like command of your own model, while being unable to reproduce your own headline ratio does not.

Finding 4 — the SHT31 cost line is 4× what you actually paid (Slide 12)

The deck says: “3× SHT31 ₹3,000” (i.e. ₹1,000 each).

Your receipt says: 3 × SHT3X at ₹250 each = ₹750.

0.3   One internal inconsistency worth tidying

Slide 9 says ±2 % RH; slide 10 says ±1.0 % RH

Both are defensible but they are different quantities, and saying them in the same breath sounds like a mistake:

  • ±2 % RH (slide 9) is the SHT31 datasheet accuracy specification — the manufacturer’s stated tolerance band.
  • ±1.0 % RH (slide 10) is the one-sigma noise we inject into the simulation’s virtual sensors.

These are consistent if you read the datasheet band as roughly two sigma, which is the normal convention. If asked: “Two percent is the datasheet accuracy band; one percent is the standard deviation we simulate, which is the same thing expressed as one sigma.”

Part AThe project from zero

Two explanations of the same thing. The first assumes nothing. The second assumes you have read the first. You need both, because judges ask for both — often in the same minute.

1   Anna-Drishti explained to a non-specialist

The 30-second version, in plain words

“Grain in an Indian warehouse is stored in sacks, stacked several metres deep. If damp gets into the middle of that stack — a roof leak, a wet sack, condensation — the grain there starts to rot from the inside. Nobody can see it, because it is buried under a metre and a half of other sacks.

Now, rotting grain breathes. The grain and the mould growing on it consume sugar and oxygen and give off carbon dioxide, water and heat — the same thing a compost heap does. That carbon dioxide seeps out through the air gaps between grains fairly quickly. The heat has to crawl through the grain itself, and grain is a very good insulator, so the heat takes far longer to reach anywhere you could measure it.

So we push thin perforated tubes into the stack, sample the air deep inside, and watch the carbon dioxide. It tells us something is going wrong roughly two weeks before a temperature probe would notice. And instead of just showing a number, we tell the warehouse manager which stack, which depth, what to do, and how many days they have.”

The four things a non-specialist needs to accept

ClaimEveryday version
Grain is alive A seed is a living thing waiting to sprout. It breathes slowly. So does anything growing on it.
Breathing makes gas, heat and water Same as a compost heap: warm in the middle, and giving off gas.
Gas travels faster than heat here Gas slips through the gaps between grains. Heat has to soak through the grains themselves, and grain insulates — that is why a sack of rice keeps its temperature for hours.
Once the toxin forms it cannot be undone You can kill the mould. You cannot un-poison the grain. So the only useful moment is before.
The one analogy that reliably lands

A compost heap. Everyone has seen one steam in the cold and smelled it from a distance. The smell reaches you across the garden long before you could feel the warmth by touching it. A spoiling pocket inside a grain stack is the same event — smaller, slower, and buried. We are standing where the smell arrives, not where the warmth does.

2   Anna-Drishti explained technically

Anna-Drishti is a depth-resolved interstitial gas and hygrometry sensing system coupled to a predictive interpretation layer, deployed in bagged-grain storage.

Three perforated probe lances are inserted between bag courses across a stack face. Each lance carries sampling ports at three depths, giving nine sampling points. A single NDIR CO₂ analyser is sequenced across all nine ports through a manifold; humidity and temperature are measured locally at each port with digital sensors.

The interpretation layer performs four conversions:

StepConversionMethod
1Relative humidity → water activity At equilibrium, interstitial RH is the grain’s water activity. No model needed — it is a definition.
2aw + temperature → days to mould onset Cardinal-value predictive mycology model for A. flavus
3CO₂ → respiration anomaly Depth-matched differential comparison across lances, in units of sigma
4All three → ranked action Composite risk score → ranked ports → action card with a deadline
The distinction that defines the project

The output is not a measurement. It is a dated, evidence-carrying decision: which stack, which depth, which action, by when, and on what basis. A system that reports 4,200 ppm has told a warehouse manager nothing actionable. A system that says “Lance 2 at 2 m, water activity 0.91, modelled mould onset in six days, act by the 30th” has.

4   Indian grain storage and the bag stack

India’s public grain storage is dominated by bagged stacks in godowns, not bulk silos. Grain sits in jute or HDPE sacks, stacked on wooden dunnage to keep it off the floor, in regular rectangular stacks several metres deep and several metres tall.

Why this geometry changes everything

PropertyBulk siloIndian bag stack
Access to interiorAuger, gravity, or fixed cables Physically obstructed by bags. Probing damages sacks.
Air pathsUniform interstitial voids Voids between kernels and between bags — more complex, less uniform
InstrumentationPermanent, per-vessel Stacks are built, moved and dismantled. Permanent fitting is uneconomic.
Commercial productsMature market Designed for the silo case, priced for it
The gap, stated precisely

There is no affordable, non-destructive, interior-resolved, predictive monitoring system designed for the bag-stack storage geometry that dominates Indian public and cooperative warehousing. Literature for the components; the gap statement itself is our synthesis.

The institutional context — why the problem is growing

Under the World’s Largest Grain Storage Plan in the Cooperative Sector, godown capacity is being built at Primary Agricultural Credit Society level, with 500+ PACS godowns identified for construction. Literature These facilities will have neither instrumentation nor trained storage scientists on site. Capacity is being distributed faster than the expertise to monitor it.

5   How a wet pocket forms, and why it accelerates

Four common origins

  1. Roof leaks during monsoon — water enters at the top of a stack.
  2. A wet consignment — grain loaded slightly above safe moisture into one corner.
  3. Condensation — day–night temperature swings drive moisture to cold surfaces: outer walls, the floor, the shaded side.
  4. Moisture migration — temperature gradients drive slow convection currents that redistribute moisture within the bulk over weeks.

Why it does not stay small — the feedback loop

Higher moisture -> faster respiration faster respiration -> releases HEAT and WATER more heat + more water -> higher moisture and higher temperature -> faster respiration (loop closes)

This is a positive feedback, which is why spoilage in grain is not linear. Small and slow for a long time, then abruptly not. In severe cases the loop runs to visible self-heating. Established

The presentational consequence

This is why early detection is worth so much more than accurate late detection. On a linear process, detecting at 50 % of the way through saves you half. On an accelerating process, detecting early saves you almost everything — and detecting late saves you almost nothing.

8   Respiration — the single reaction everything rests on

The reaction

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 -> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + energy (heat) glucose oxygen carbon water dioxide

Both the grain itself (a living seed) and any fungi growing on it run this reaction. It is ordinary aerobic respiration. Established

Why this single equation carries the whole project

CO₂, water and heat are not three independent phenomena that happen to occur together. They are three products of one reaction, in fixed stoichiometric proportion. That is what licenses using one of them as a proxy for the others — and it is the answer to “why should CO₂ tell you anything about heat or spoilage?”

9   Moisture, humidity and water activity

Slide 5’s headline is “Moisture is the total. aw is the spendable part.” This is the single most important concept in the biology, and the one most likely to be probed.

Three quantities people confuse

QuantityDefinitionWhat it tells you
Moisture content
(% wet basis)		 Mass of water ÷ total mass, as a percentage. What a moisture meter reads. How much water is present in total — including water chemically bound to starch and protein that no organism can use.
Relative humidity
(% RH)		 Water vapour pressure in air ÷ saturation vapour pressure, as a percentage. The state of the air, not the grain — until they reach equilibrium.
Water activity
(aw, 0–1)		 The free fraction of water — chemically available, not bound. What organisms actually respond to. This is the biologically meaningful quantity.
The equivalence the whole sensing strategy depends on

At equilibrium, the relative humidity of the air between the kernels IS the water activity of the grain. aw = RH ÷ 100.

This is not a model or an approximation we invented — it is what water activity means. It is why a ₹250 humidity sensor can measure the variable that governs fungal growth, and why we do not need a laboratory water-activity meter in the field. Established

Why the same moisture behaves differently at different temperatures

Temperature changes how tightly water is held by the starch and protein matrix. Warmer grain releases a larger free fraction at the same total moisture. This is why slide 5’s table has both a moisture column and a temperature column — and why quoting a moisture percentage alone is scientifically incomplete.

The sorption isotherm — converting between them

We use the modified Chung–Pfost equation with published ASAE D245 coefficients for shelled corn: Literature

Slide 5’s table is this equation evaluated at five points. All five reproduce exactly (§0.2). Why this matters rhetorically: we did not invent a conversion. We used a published standard with published coefficients for the specific commodity in our jars.

Part DThe physics: spoilage breathes

Slide 3 is the intellectual centre of the project. This part explains why it is true, how the numbers were produced, and exactly how much weight each one can carry.

11   Why gas arrives first

All three signals — CO₂, heat, moisture — leave the same pocket at the same moment. They arrive at a sensor at wildly different times because they travel by different physical mechanisms through different media.

SignalTravels byThrough whatWhy fast or slow
CO₂Molecular diffusion of a gas The connected air space between kernels Fast. Gas molecules move freely through open, connected voids. Roughly 40 % of a grain bed by volume is air.
HeatThermal conduction The kernels themselves, plus trapped air Slow. Grain is a good thermal insulator, and the trapped air pockets that help gas move actually hinder conduction.
MoistureVapour diffusion + sorption Air, but repeatedly absorbed by grain Slowest. Every kernel it passes acts as a sponge, absorbing and re-releasing it. The grain buffers moisture heavily.
The elegant part — and worth saying out loud

The same property that makes grain a good insulator (trapped air between kernels) is what makes it a good conductor of gas. Porosity helps CO₂ and hinders heat. They are not two unrelated facts — they are the same fact seen from two sides. Established

12   Fick’s law — how gas moves

Intuitively

Put a drop of ink in still water. It spreads out, from where there is a lot to where there is little, and it never spontaneously re-concentrates. The steeper the difference in concentration, the faster it spreads. That is diffusion, and Fick’s law is just that sentence written in symbols.

Technically

Fick's First Law J = -D dC/dx J = flux (how much gas crosses unit area per second) D = diffusivity (m^2/s - how easily the medium lets it through) dC/dx = concentration gradient minus = flow goes DOWN the gradient, high to low

For how a concentration evolves over time, Fick’s second law applies. The practical consequence we use is the characteristic diffusion time:

t = L^2 / D t = time to travel distance L Note the SQUARE. Doubling the distance quadruples the time.
The one insight to take from this equation

Because distance is squared, diffusion is fast over short distances and punishingly slow over long ones. This is exactly why a probe placed inside the stack works and a sensor on the outside does not — not because the outside sensor is worse, but because it is asking diffusion to do something it is bad at.

13   Fourier’s law — how heat moves

Intuitively

Hold a metal spoon in hot tea and the handle warms in seconds. Hold a wooden spoon and it barely warms at all. Same heat, same geometry — different material. Grain behaves like the wooden spoon.

Technically

Fourier's Law q = -k dT/dx q = heat flux k = thermal conductivity (W/m.K) dT/dx = temperature gradient Thermal diffusivity alpha = k / (rho x cp) rho = density, cp = specific heat capacity alpha has the SAME units as D (m^2/s) - which is what lets us compare gas transport and heat transport directly.
Why the comparison in slide 3 is legitimate

A judge may reasonably ask whether comparing a gas diffusivity with a thermal diffusivity is apples-to-oranges. It is not. Both have units of m²/s, and both obey a t = L²/D scaling law. They are mathematically the same kind of quantity describing the same kind of spreading process. That is precisely why the ratio is meaningful. Established

17   Why CO₂ — and why CO₂ alone is not enough

The case for CO₂

  1. It is upstream. CO₂ is a direct product of the reaction. Temperature is an accumulated consequence of that reaction — heat must build up faster than it dissipates before a probe registers anything.
  2. It travels through the fast medium. Interstitial air, not insulating kernels.
  3. It integrates all sources. Grain, fungal and insect respiration all produce it. One signal covers three failure modes.
  4. Its baseline is low. Rising from ~400 ppm to thousands is a large relative change and therefore easy to detect — unlike O₂, where you hunt a small dip in a large number.

The honest case against — and how we answer it

ObjectionOur answer
CO₂ is not specific. It cannot tell you whether the source is grain, fungus or insects. Correct, and we do not claim otherwise. That is precisely why CO₂ is not used alone. It tells us where and that; water activity tells us whether fungal growth is possible there; the combination is what produces a defensible recommendation.
Ventilation changes CO₂. Which is why we never use an absolute threshold. Each port is compared against the other lances at its own depth on the same analyser. A ventilation change affects all ports similarly and cancels in the differential.
Ambient CO₂ drifts. Same answer — differential, not absolute. Plus a rolling per-port baseline.
Sensors drift. One shared analyser reads all nine ports, so drift is common-mode and cancels in port-to-port comparison. This is the main engineering reason for the shared-analyser architecture, and it is worth stating — most people assume it is only about cost.
The answer to “why CO₂?” in one paragraph

“Because it is the earliest signal that physically escapes the pocket. Spoilage is respiration — sugar and oxygen in, CO₂, water and heat out. All three leave together, but CO₂ moves through the air gaps between kernels while heat has to conduct through the kernels themselves, and grain is an insulator. We are not detecting a different event earlier; we are detecting the same event through the faster channel.”

Recommendation, and the reasoning

NDIR, single shared analyser, multiplexed across nine ports.

  • NDIR because it is genuinely selective to CO₂ and stable over years. In buried grain you cannot recalibrate weekly, so drift and consumable chemistry are disqualifying.
  • Wide range matters: a spoiling pocket in a sealed environment can reach tens of thousands of ppm. A sensor capped at 5,000 ppm saturates and tells you nothing. This is why the deck specifies an SCD30-class part (0–40,000 ppm) rather than a cheaper 5,000 ppm module.
  • Shared, not per-port, for two reasons — cost, and more importantly drift cancellation. Nine separate sensors would each drift independently, and port-to-port comparison would become meaningless. One instrument reading all nine means every comparison is made against the same baseline by the same hardware.
The firmware trap that would silently destroy the whole project

Every NDIR sensor ships with Automatic Baseline Correction (ABC) enabled. ABC assumes the sensor periodically sees fresh outdoor air at ~400 ppm, and silently re-zeros itself to the lowest value it has seen over a rolling window.

Buried in grain, the sensor never sees fresh air. ABC would therefore progressively re-zero the elevated interstitial CO₂ to “normal” — erasing precisely the signal we exist to detect, while continuing to report confident-looking numbers.

ABC must be disabled in firmware. Slide 9 calls this out, and it is one of the strongest “we have actually thought about this” details in the deck. Make sure Vishal can explain it — a judge with embedded experience will recognise it as a real practitioner’s concern rather than a textbook answer.

The sensor must be in gas communication with the interstitial air while being protected from dust, liquid water, condensation and mechanical damage. These requirements conflict, and resolving them is real engineering.

25   Fungal biology for this project

Why Aspergillus flavus specifically

  • It is the canonical aflatoxin producer on maize — the classic pairing in the literature, and a documented Indian problem.
  • It is a storage fungus, not a field fungus — it grows at the relatively low water activities found in stored grain, which is exactly our regime.
  • Its cardinal values are published, so a growth model can be built on citable numbers rather than invented ones.

The cardinal-value model, in plain terms

A cardinal model says an organism has a minimum, an optimum and a maximum for each environmental variable, and growth rate falls off from the optimum towards the limits. Our model uses:

VariableMinOptimumMax
Temperature12 °C33 °C43 °C
Water activity0.820.99—

Growth rate is the product of a temperature factor and a water-activity factor, scaled by a maximum rate. Days-to-onset is then simply the reciprocal. Engineering est. — standard approach, our coefficients.

Krittika’s core explanation, in one paragraph

“Fungi do not respond to how much water is present. They respond to how much is available. Some water in a kernel is chemically bound to starch and protein and no organism can access it. Water activity is the free fraction, on a scale of zero to one. Below about 0.82, A. flavus cannot extract enough water to grow — and because relative humidity at equilibrium is water activity, we can measure the governing variable with an ordinary humidity sensor.”

Why “three consecutive readings” matters

A single noisy sample must never raise an alarm. Requiring persistence trades a small amount of latency (three hours at hourly sampling) for a large reduction in false alarms. In a system whose value depends on being trusted, a false alarm is more expensive than three hours.

Q7What is water activity, in one sentence?
Short The fraction of the water in the grain that is chemically free and therefore usable by an organism.
Technical Some water is bound to starch and protein and unavailable. Water activity is the free fraction on a 0–1 scale, and it equals the equilibrium relative humidity of the surrounding air. That equivalence is what lets a humidity sensor measure the biologically governing variable.

Technical Two batches at identical moisture content behave differently at different temperatures, because temperature changes how tightly the matrix holds water. Moisture content also requires a physical sample, so it cannot be continuous or interior.

Technical The organism can survive and grow in conditions where it does not produce aflatoxin. This is why we model two thresholds — growth at 0.82, toxin at 0.85 — rather than one.

Technical Grain is a living seed and respires at a low baseline rate. Above the water-activity threshold, fungal respiration dominates and rises steeply. Insects respire too. Technical Using ASAE D245 corn coefficients means our humidity-to-water- activity conversion is a published standard, not something we invented. It is also what is in our jars — one commodity validated properly rather than four claimed loosely.
Q13Would this work for wheat or rice?
Short Yes, but it needs that commodity’s isotherm coefficients.
Technical The physics and the detector are commodity-independent. Only the humidity-to-water-activity conversion is commodity-specific, and it is parameterised, so a new commodity is a configuration change rather than a redesign. The system should refuse to report aw for an unconfigured commodity rather than silently using the wrong curve.
Q14Your respiration coefficients — where are they from?
Short The functional form is standard; the coefficients are ours, tuned to reproduce published safe-storage timelines.
Technical Exponential in moisture and Q₁₀ in temperature is the standard form. Our R₀ = 0.5 mg CO₂/kg/day at 14 % and 25 °C, k = 0.7, Q₁₀ = 2.5. These are an engineering estimate, on Krittika’s sign-off sheet.
Never Present them as measured values.
Q15Is self-heating real, or theoretical?
Short Real, and well documented in stored-grain practice.
Technical It is a positive feedback: moisture raises respiration, respiration releases heat and water, which raise respiration further. In severe cases stacks heat substantially. It is also why spoilage is non-linear and why early detection is worth disproportionately more than late detection.

CO₂ and the physics

Q16Why CO₂ rather than temperature?
Short Because it is the same event through a faster channel.
Technical CO₂ and heat come from one reaction, but CO₂ travels through the connected interstitial air while heat must conduct through the kernels, and grain is an insulator. Temperature is also an accumulated consequence — heat must build faster than it dissipates before a probe registers anything.
Q17How do you know CO₂ arrives first?
Short From the diffusivities — and we have not yet measured it ourselves.
Technical Gas diffusivity in interstitial air is around 10⁻⁶ m²/s; bulk grain thermal diffusivity around 10⁻⁷. Both scale as t = L²/D, so the ratio is about 17 at any distance. CO₂ is also identified in storage-technology literature as an earlier indicator than temperature. Our specific lead-time figure is modelled, not measured, and demonstrating it is the point of our validation experiment.
Never Say “we measured a fifteen-times lead”.
Q18Where does the 15× figure come from?
Short The ratio of the two diffusivities. It is 16.7; we round it down.
Technical 15.43 days for heat over 0.4 m divided by 0.93 days for CO₂ gives 16.7. Because both times scale with length squared, the distance cancels — the ratio holds at any separation, which is what makes it a robust claim even though the absolute times depend on assumed geometry.
Q19Is comparing gas diffusivity with thermal diffusivity legitimate?
Short Yes — both have units of m²/s and obey the same scaling law.
Technical Thermal diffusivity α = k/(ρcp) has exactly the same dimensions as mass diffusivity D. Both describe spreading, both give t = L²/D. It is a mathematically valid comparison, not an analogy.
Q20Explain Fick’s law.
Short Gas flows from where there is a lot to where there is little, faster when the difference is steeper.
Technical J = −D dC/dx. Flux is proportional to the concentration gradient, negative because flow is down-gradient. The practical consequence is t = L²/D — distance squared, which is why interior placement matters so much.
Q21Explain Fourier’s law.
Short Heat flows down a temperature gradient, at a rate set by the material.
Technical q = −k dT/dx. Grain has low thermal conductivity, and the trapped air that helps gas move actually hinders conduction — the same porosity works in opposite directions for the two signals.
Q22Why is moisture so much slower than gas?
Short Because every kernel it passes absorbs some of it.
Technical Water vapour diffuses through air at a rate comparable to CO₂, but grain is a powerful moisture buffer. Vapour is repeatedly sorbed and desorbed, which enormously retards net transport. Our effective coefficient lumps that together and is the weakest of our three transport numbers.
Q23What if the godown is ventilated?
Short It affects all ports similarly, and our comparison is differential.
Technical We never use an absolute threshold. Each port is compared against other lances at the same depth on the same analyser, so a facility-wide ventilation change is common-mode and cancels. A single anomalous port survives that cancellation; a ventilation change does not.
Q24What if ambient CO₂ drifts?
Short Same answer — differential, plus a rolling per-port baseline.
Technical Seasonal or diurnal ambient change moves every port together. Only a local source produces port-to-port divergence at a given depth.
Q25How do you know the CO₂ is from spoilage and not something else?
Short We do not, from CO₂ alone — which is why we measure water activity too.
Technical CO₂ localises and flags. Water activity tells us whether fungal growth is even possible at that location. If CO₂ is elevated and aw is below the growth floor, that is a respiration anomaly without a fungal explanation — possibly insects, possibly grain respiration — and our action card says so rather than claiming mould.

Sensors

Q26Why NDIR rather than a cheaper sensor?
Short Selectivity and long-term stability.
Technical Electrochemical cells consume reagent and have finite life; metal-oxide sensors are not CO₂-specific at all and drift heavily. Buried in grain you cannot recalibrate frequently, so drift and consumables are disqualifying. NDIR measures a real optical absorption specific to CO₂.
Q27What is ABC and why does it matter?
Short Automatic Baseline Correction — and it would silently erase our signal.
Technical NDIR sensors assume they periodically see fresh 400 ppm air and re-zero to the lowest recent value. Buried in grain they never do, so ABC would progressively re-zero elevated interstitial CO₂ to “normal” while still reporting confident numbers. It must be disabled in firmware. This is the single most dangerous default in the whole system.
Q28What happens if the sensor gets wet?
Short Hydrophobic membrane, plus the on-chip heater to clear condensation.
Technical Condensation pinning RH near 100 % is the most likely field failure. The SHT3x has an integrated heater that can be pulsed to drive off condensate, and we reject readings taken during a transient. Electronics are potted and isolated from the gas path.
Q29How do you stop dust blocking the port?
Short Sintered filter plus several small perforations rather than one hole.
Technical Multiple small ports resist single-kernel blockage; a sintered element and PTFE membrane pass gas but stop fines. Firmware also watches for a port that goes flat while its peers vary, which is the signature of a blockage rather than a genuine reading.
Q30±2 % RH is ±0.02 aw. Your thresholds are 0.03 apart. Is that good enough?
Short It is tight, and we treat 0.82 as a conservative trigger rather than a precise boundary.
Technical This is a genuine limitation and worth stating. We mitigate three ways: take the conservative end of the threshold range; require the CO₂ anomaly to agree before escalating; and calibrate against saturated salt standards that bracket the decision region. A higher-grade sensor (±1.5 %) is a drop-in upgrade if precision becomes limiting.
Never Claim the measurement is more precise than the sensor allows.
Q31How will you calibrate in the field?
Short Saturated salt solutions for humidity; a reference gas or fresh-air check for CO₂.
Technical Saturated NaCl holds 0.753 aw, KCl 0.843, K₂SO₄ 0.973 — a two-point calibration bracketing our decision region, using reagents any lab has. For CO₂, a manual fresh-air reference with ABC disabled, performed on a schedule rather than automatically.
Q32Why one analyser rather than nine sensors?
Short Cost, and more importantly drift cancellation.
Technical Nine sensors would each drift independently, so port-to-port comparison — the basis of our whole detector — would become unreliable. One instrument reading all nine means every comparison is against the same baseline by the same hardware. It also removes the most fragile component from the buried environment.

ESP32 and electronics

Q33Why ESP32 and not a Raspberry Pi?
Short Reliability and power. A Pi runs an OS that can corrupt on power loss.
Technical A sensing node must survive unattended power interruption. A microcontroller resumes; an OS may not. The ESP32 also deep-sleeps at sub-milliamp, has integrated Wi-Fi and BLE, two I²C controllers, and 3.3 V logic matching all our sensors — at around ₹500.
Q34Why not an Arduino Uno?
Short No networking and 2 KB of RAM.
Technical We buffer readings when offline and compute an anomaly score locally. Neither is practical on an 8-bit part with 2 KB RAM and no radio.
Q35How do three sensors share one I²C bus if they have two addresses?
Short A TCA9548A multiplexer — or the ESP32’s second I²C controller.
Technical The SHT3x has only 0x44 and 0x45. Three on one bus collide. The multiplexer gives eight isolated channels and scales to nine sensors; alternatively the two hardware I²C controllers give two independent buses. Knowing both routes matters — the multiplexer is the scalable answer.
Q36I²C over two metres is out of spec. How do you handle that?
Short Lower the clock, strengthen the pull-ups, use shielded cable.
Technical Drop to 100 kHz, use 2.2 kΩ pull-ups instead of 4.7 k, and shielded twisted pair. If it remains unreliable at full lance length the correct fix is a small microcontroller at each depth talking RS-485 — but we would measure the problem before designing for it.
Q37What if there is no network?
Short The system is offline-first by design.
Technical The node computes its own anomaly score and can raise a local alarm with no connectivity at all. Readings buffer to flash and synchronise opportunistically. Rural godown connectivity cannot be assumed, so the decision logic never depends on a network.
Q38How is it powered?
Short Mains where available, with deep-sleep duty cycling; solar is the field option.
Technical We sample hourly, not continuously, so the node sleeps at sub-milliamp between cycles. The supply must be sized for the simultaneous peak of Wi-Fi transmission and the analyser, not the average, or the node browns out during upload.

Mechanical and probe

Q39How do you insert a 2 m probe without damaging bags?
Short Between bag courses, with a conical tip that parts grain rather than coring it.
Technical 20–25 mm diameter is a deliberate compromise — thin enough to pass between courses, thick enough to resist buckling under insertion load. The lance is removable and reusable across stacks, which matters because stacks are rebuilt.
Q40How do you stop the three depth ports mixing inside the tube?
Short Internal seals between depth zones.
Technical Without them, all three ports share one internal volume and every port reads the average — destroying the depth resolution the whole detector depends on. This is the single most important internal detail of the lance.
Q41What about dead volume in the tubing?
Short We purge each port before dwelling on it.
Technical Otherwise you measure the previous port’s gas. Purge time is set by tube volume and pump rate, and is exactly the kind of question a CFD chamber-flow simulation answers with a real number rather than a guess.
Q42Is CFD actually useful here or is it decoration?
Short Useful for two specific questions; decoration for anything larger.
Technical Chamber purge time and CO₂ transport from a pocket through porous grain to a port are both real design questions with real answers. A full 3D transient simulation of an entire godown would be dominated by porosity and boundary assumptions we cannot verify — a colourful picture and no added confidence.
Never Claim CFD proves the system works. It validates a model, not reality.
Q43What material, and why?
Short Stainless 304 for production; aluminium for the prototype.
Technical Corrosion resistance in humid grain, food-contact acceptability, and stiffness for insertion. Aluminium is cheaper and lighter for a first article where we are testing geometry rather than durability.

Software, detection and AI

Q44Where is the AI?
Short There is none, deliberately.
Technical We have nine sensors and no labelled failure dataset. Training a model on that would produce something we could neither explain nor defend. Every alert carries its own reasoning — this port, this many sigma above its depth peers, this water activity, this many days. When the validation programme produces a labelled dataset, machine learning becomes worth revisiting.
Never Call the risk score “AI”. It is a weighted sum, and saying so is stronger.
Q45Why not a simple threshold?
Short Because there is no correct absolute value.
Technical Godown conditions swing seasonally, and so does baseline respiration. Any fixed limit either cries wolf in summer or sleeps through winter. We compare each port against its own depth peers instead.
Q46Why compare within a depth rather than across all nine ports?
Short Depth stratification is real — about 1,000 ppm.
Technical Deep grain is furthest from a venting face and naturally reads highest. Pooling all nine ports buries a genuine local anomaly inside that natural spread. We found this in simulation: comparing all nine, the signal disappeared entirely.
Q47What is your false-positive rate?
Short In simulation, clean grain peaks at 1.5 sigma against a 3.0 sigma threshold — but we have no field data.
Technical The persistence rule (three consecutive readings) suppresses single-sample noise. A real false-positive rate requires field deployment across seasons, and we would not claim one before we have it.
Never Quote a false-positive percentage. You do not have one.
Q48Why simulate sensor noise at all?
Short So the detector is proven on imperfect data rather than idealised data.
Technical We inject datasheet-accurate noise — CO₂ ±25 ppm plus 1.5 % of reading, RH ±1 %, temperature ±0.15 °C — into every virtual reading. A detector tuned on clean data would fail on first contact with a real sensor.
Q49How do you fuse the signals?
Short A weighted composite — 35 % water activity, 40 % urgency, 25 % CO₂ anomaly.
Technical Each term is inspectable, so when an alert fires we can say precisely which component drove it. The weights are an assumption, not a derived result, and are on the verification sheet.
Q50What if two signals disagree?
Short That is informative, and the action card says so.
Technical Elevated CO₂ with aw below the growth floor means respiration without a fungal explanation — possibly insects, possibly a nearby source the port cannot resolve. The recommendation becomes “targeted probe” rather than “aerate for mould”. Disagreement changes the action rather than being suppressed.

Validation and evidence

Q51What have you actually demonstrated?
Short A measured water-activity difference through real hardware and real code, plus a working simulation.
Technical Two sealed jars, SHT3x on the field firmware, readings into the same decision engine the simulation uses. Dry jar below the growth floor, wet jar above with a predicted onset. We have not observed spoilage and we have not measured CO₂.
Q52So none of this is real?
Short The physics is simulated; the decision engine is production code; the water activity was measured.
Technical Be precise about which layer is which. The transport model is simulation. The interpretation pipeline is the same code that ran on our jar reading. The hardware exists and works. What is missing is the CO₂ sensor and field time.
Q53How would you prove the CO₂ lead time?
Short Two instrumented grain columns, one with an induced wet pocket, logged continuously.
Technical CO₂, RH and temperature at multiple depths, sampled every 15–60 minutes for two to four weeks, against an identical control column. Acceptance criterion: CO₂ departs from control measurably earlier than temperature. That experiment is designed and not yet run — it needs the sensor.
Q54Your jars were sealed 15 hours. Isn’t that meaningless?
Short For spoilage, yes. For water activity, no.
Technical Sorption equilibrium between grain and headspace air happens in hours, so the aw measurement is valid. Fungal growth takes days, so we make no claim about spoilage. That is exactly why slide 11 separates the two.
Q55Is your baseline comparison fair?
Short We deliberately made it generous, and we can show you how.
Technical Fortnightly inspection when many facilities are monthly. Inspectors never miss visible mould. And we added a musty-smell detection route specifically because our first model had conventional inspection never finding the pocket, which looked rigged. Day 42 exists because we handicapped ourselves.
Q56Where does 0.4 m spear reach come from?
Short It is our estimate of a bag spear’s practical reach, and it needs a citable source.
Technical This is an assumption on the verification sheet. If it is too generous our comparison looks better than it should; if too harsh, worse. We believe it is realistic, and confirming it against a standard or a physical measurement is on our list.
Never Present it as a published figure.

Cost, scale and commercial

Q57What does one unit cost?
Short Around ₹8,000–10,500 for the prototype, inside our ₹19,800 budget.
Technical The NDIR analyser dominates at around ₹5,000. That is exactly why one analyser serves nine ports rather than nine analysers serving nine ports.
Q58Can this scale economically?
Short The expensive component is shared, so cost per monitored point falls with port count.
Technical Adding ports costs tubing and a manifold position, not another analyser. Our scale estimates are extrapolations from component pricing, not quotations — treat them as indicative.
Q59Who pays for this?
Short The facility, justified by avoided loss and reduced fumigation.
Technical The honest answer is that a business case requires a pilot producing documented lead-time and chemical-use figures against that facility’s existing practice. We have designed that pilot; we have not run it.
Q60Why can’t a commercial system do this?
Short They can — for silos, at silo prices.
Technical Commercial grain monitoring is capable and well engineered. Our claim is not that they cannot work; it is that they are designed for bulk vessels and priced for large operations, and Indian public storage is bagged stacks at PACS scale.
Never Claim competitors are technically incapable.

Deployment and practicality

Q61How many lances per stack?
Short Three across a 4 m face, three depths each.
Technical That spacing is a design choice awaiting the coverage analysis — the study that determines what percentage of hotspots three lances at that spacing detect, and how quickly. It is on our list and not yet complete.
Q62What about a pocket between lances?
Short That is a genuine coverage limitation.
Technical Diffusive reach determines the detection radius around each port. A pocket far enough from every port produces too small a differential. That is exactly what the coverage analysis quantifies, and the answer determines lance spacing.
Q63Who installs and maintains it?
Short Insertion is designed to be repeatable by an unskilled operator.
Technical The lance is removable and reusable. Maintenance is filter replacement and periodic calibration. The analyser lives in an accessible head unit rather than buried, specifically so it can be serviced.
Q64What if the operator ignores the alert?
Short Then nothing improves — which is why the output is an action, not a number.
Technical An action card naming stack, depth, action and deadline is far more actionable than a ppm reading. The tamper-evident log also creates accountability: what was flagged, when, and what was done.
Q65Does it work during fumigation?
Short That is when verification matters most.
Technical Post-treatment CO₂ decay is our efficacy signal. If the anomaly does not fall after treatment, the system escalates rather than assuming success — which is the failure mode current practice never detects.

Safety, ethics and limitations

Q66Are you culturing aflatoxin-producing fungi?
Short No. We use naturally occurring colonisation of wetted grain, in the lab, under supervision.
Technical No deliberate inoculation of toxigenic strains. Jars remain sealed outside the lab, handled with gloves and a dust mask, and autoclaved or disposed of per protocol. Deliberate culturing would require containment we do not claim to have.
Q67What is the worst failure mode of your system?
Short A false negative that creates false confidence.
Technical A blocked port reading flat looks like healthy grain. That is worse than no system at all, because it displaces vigilance. We detect it by watching for ports that stop varying while peers vary — but it is the failure we take most seriously.
Never Claim the system cannot miss anything.
Q68What are you most likely to be wrong about?
Short The absolute lead time. The ordering we are confident about; the magnitude is modelled.
Technical Real grain beds have convection, non-uniform porosity and bag boundaries our model treats simply. Those could compress or extend the gap. The physics that gas outruns heat is robust; the specific number of days is not.
Q69What happens if your prediction is wrong?
Short The recommended first action is aeration — reversible, non-toxic and cheap.
Technical The tier structure is designed so a false positive costs very little: you aerate a zone that did not need it. Only confirmed, escalated cases reach fumigation. The asymmetry between the cost of a false alarm and the cost of a missed event is what justifies alerting early.

Technical The individual signals are established science and we say so. What is new is the combination applied to this geometry, the single-analyser design that makes depth-resolved mapping affordable while cancelling drift, and the shift from reporting a reading to issuing a dated recommendation with verification.
Q71Isn’t this just integration of existing parts?
Short Substantially yes — and the integration is the contribution.
Technical Conceding this is stronger than denying it. NDIR, isotherms and growth models all exist. Nobody has combined them for bagged Indian storage at a PACS-affordable price point with a closed verification loop. Naming honestly which parts are novel and which are integration is what makes the novel claims believable.

Short Because the market that needs it cannot pay silo prices.
Technical The technology is not the barrier; the economics are. Commercial development targets large operations with capital budgets. The shared-analyser architecture is our attempt to make the same capability affordable at PACS scale.

39   The mathematics, verbally defensible

39.1   Diffusion — the one equation to memorise

t = L^2 / D Worked, for CO2 over 0.4 m: t = (0.4)^2 / (2.0e-6) = 0.16 / 0.000002 = 80,000 s = 0.93 days Same L, for heat (alpha = 1.2e-7): t = 0.16 / 0.00000012 = 1,333,333 s = 15.4 days Ratio = 15.4 / 0.93 = 16.7

If you can do this on a whiteboard, you own slide 3. The key point to add: because L is squared in both, it cancels in the ratio — so 16.7 holds at any distance, even though the absolute days depend on the geometry you assume.

39.2   Mass balance — CO₂ accumulation

Rate of change = production - removal dC/dt = (R x rho_bulk) / (porosity x conversion) - k_vent x (C - C_ambient) At steady state, dC/dt = 0, so: C_steady = C_ambient + production / k_vent

The useful intuition: a pocket does not raise CO₂ without limit. It rises until production balances loss to the venting faces. That equilibrium level, not an unbounded climb, is what the sensor sees.

39.3   Stoichiometry — converting CO₂ to grain lost

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 -> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O 180 g glucose -> 264 g CO2 So 1 g dry matter lost -> 1.467 g CO2 1% dry matter loss -> 14.67 g CO2 per kg grain

This is chemistry, not a model parameter. It lets accumulated CO₂ be converted directly into grain actually lost — which is how the system reports dry-matter loss.

39.4   The isotherm

aw = exp( -(A / (T + C)) x exp( -B x M_db ) ) A = 620.7, B = 0.2118, C = 52.15 (shelled corn, ASAE D245) Check: M = 14% wb -> 16.28% db, T = 25 C exp(-0.2118 x 16.28) = 0.0318 620.7 / (25 + 52.15) = 8.045 aw = exp(-8.045 x 0.0318) = 0.774 [matches slide 5]

39.5   Growth rate and days to onset

mu = mu_opt x f(aw) x f(T) f(aw) = (aw - 0.82) / (0.99 - 0.82), clamped 0..1 f(T) = 1 - ((T - 33)/w)^2, clamped 0..1 mu_opt = 0.20 /day days_to_onset = 1 / mu Check: aw 0.87, T 30 C f(aw) = 0.05/0.17 = 0.294 f(T) = 1 - (3/21)^2 = 0.980 mu = 0.20 x 0.294 x 0.980 = 0.0576 days = 1/0.0576 = 17.4 [matches slide 5]

39.6   The anomaly score

For port i at depth d: peers = other ports at the same depth z_i = (reading_i - mean(peers)) / spread(peers) ALERT if z_i >= 3.0 for 3 consecutive readings

The problem

Indian grain sits in bagged stacks metres deep. Damp pockets form inside from roof leaks, wet consignments or condensation. The interior cannot be inspected — a spear reaches ~0.4 m. Spoilage becomes visible only after it is irreversible, and aflatoxin cannot be undone by drying or cooking. Losses assessed at 3.89–5.92 % (NABCONS, to Parliament, 2024). 500+ PACS godowns under construction, none instrumented.

The insight

Spoilage is respiration: sugar + oxygen → CO₂ + water + heat, from one reaction. The three products travel at wildly different speeds. Over 0.4 m: CO₂ 0.9 days, heat 15.4 days, moisture 123.5 days. Gas leads by ~17×. We stand where the signal arrives first.

The science

Fungi respond to water activity, not moisture — the free, usable fraction. At equilibrium, interstitial RH is aw. A. flavus growth floor 0.82 (conservative end of a published range); toxin needs 0.85. Crossing point is about 15 % moisture. Above it, the clock starts — and the clock is what we report.

The solution

Sense (9 ports, 3 lances × 3 depths, one shared NDIR analyser) → Interpret (RH→aw→growth forecast) → Decide (composite risk, ranked ports, action card with deadline) → Verify (post-treatment signature decay). Output is a port, a reason and a deadline — not an alarm.

The hardware

SCD30-class NDIR (0–40,000 ppm, ABC disabled), 3× SHT3x, ESP32, TCA9548A multiplexer. All 3.3 V I²C by design. ~₹8,000–10,500, inside the ₹19,800 budget. Offline-first.

The detector

Not a fixed threshold (seasonal swings) and not all nine ports pooled (1,000 ppm depth stratification buries the signal). Depth-matched differential — each port against other lances at its own depth, same analyser, cancelling drift and ventilation together. Clean grain 1.5σ, alert at 3.0σ, hotspot 4.2σ, sustained 3 readings.

The novelty

Signals are established science. New: bag-stack geometry, single-analyser drift cancellation, prediction rather than measurement, closed verification loop.

The validation

Have: two sealed maize jars, SHT3x on field firmware, reading into the same decision engine, dry jar below 0.82 and wet jar above with a predicted onset; a self-checking digital twin. Do not have: observed spoilage, any CO₂ measurement, field lead-time data, the coverage result.

The four things never to say

1. “A. flavus physically cannot grow below 0.82.” Say: conservative end of a published range.

2. “We measured a 15× lead time.” Say: our model gives ~17× from published diffusivities; we have not measured it.

3. “We watched grain spoil” or “we measured CO₂”. Neither is true.

4. “Existing systems can’t do this.” They can — for silos, at silo prices. The argument is geometry and economics.

The three sentences that win the room

The insight: “The same biological event announces itself as gas about two weeks before it announces itself as warmth. We chose to stand where it arrives first.”

The honesty: “We do not measure toxin. We predict the conditions that create it, and we say so in every output the system produces.”
